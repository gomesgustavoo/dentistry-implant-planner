Trained here

The models I trained, and the evidence

The model catalog lists seven models. Five are third‑party and labeled as such, with what each is measured to do and whether it is installed. I trained the other two: the network that segments all 47 structures, and a second one built for the three canals an anterior implant has to clear.

The base network

nnU‑Net with a U‑Mamba2 bottleneck

Fine‑tuned on ToothFairy3 over 512 cases for 1,000 epochs, and scored on a 20‑case held‑out set it never saw, by the same pipeline that serves predictions.

  • Challenge Dice0.8965
  • Strict Dice0.8292
  • HD951.235 mm
  • NSD0.9736
  • Inferior alveolar canal, Dice (left / right)0.9008 / 0.9009

Above the published SegResNet entry (0.87) and within 0.012 of the ToothFairy3 challenge winners (0.908) — reached on a 12 GB card at 0.36× their patch volume.

The training run

Clean for all 1,000 epochs

Validation loss tracked training loss the whole way: no overfitting on 512 cases, and the pseudo‑Dice moving average was still rising at epoch 1,000. It had not saturated, and the winners trained for 1,500. checkpoint_final beat checkpoint_best on every metric (challenge Dice 0.8887 against 0.8849, under the earlier component filter), which is what a fully annealed poly schedule should do.

A second network, for a reason

The anterior canal specialist

The inferior alveolar canal ends at the mental foramen, so an implant in front of it has three other canals to clear. A network trained on the anterior mandible alone gains +0.0505 / +0.0874 / +0.0172 Dice on the left incisive, right incisive, and lingual canals, pulls their predicted volume from 155–205% of ground truth to 99–111%, and beats the base model on 40 external PMCanalSeg cases at p = 7.6 × 10−4.

The finding that was worth the most

A threshold table that was quietly all‑ones

Small‑island removal works per class: a fragment is dropped when it is smaller than that structure has ever legitimately been. Rebuilding that table by taking the 2nd percentile over every connected component in the training annotations produced a threshold of one voxel for everything — because ground truth is not clean: the lower jawbone alone has 17,733 components across 512 cases, about 34 per case where the anatomy has one.

Taking the largest component per case instead spans 44 voxels (lingual canal) to 881,756 (lower jawbone). The difference is not cosmetic:

  • Rebuilt filter0.8292 strict · 0.8965 challenge
Published because it failed

A negative result, with the threshold set first

Three head and neck models were evaluated for an extended taxonomy of 42 structures beyond the dental set. Across three held‑out cases, that is 126 structure‑opportunities, and the plausibility gate was committed before anything was measured. One survived it.

The tongue came out at 1.76 cm³ against an anatomical 70–100 cm³. One masseter was found and the other was not, inside a field of view containing both. The oropharynx arrived in 155 connected pieces. All three, and the CT‑trained TotalSegmentator teeth model with them, were removed from the app: a model measured not to work on cone‑beam CT is not a choice worth offering.

What a held‑out set cannot tell you

Stated before the numbers, because it decides what they mean: this held‑out set cannot grant ownership of a structure to me or to anyone else. The third‑party models were trained on the same public release, so only a loss is evidence. Measured that way on 18 of the held‑out cases, TotalSegmentator earned ownership of nothing: zero accessory‑canal voxels in 18 of 18, −0.078 Dice on the pulp, and −0.014 and −0.021 on the two inferior alveolar canals.

Deployed here

And it runs in production, on one GPU

Training a network is half of the work. This one has an API in front of it, a queue behind it, single sign‑on, per‑tenant isolation, and quotas, and it has been serving real uploads on the public internet since August 2026.

  • GPU per scan, two models~98 s
  • Base network95.0 s
  • Canal specialist2.5 s
  • Structures per pass47
  • Tooth classes, FDI32

Those seconds are published in the model catalog before you upload a byte, next to what each model is measured to do, because “which model should segment my teeth?” is a question a user is about to be asked, and a model name does not answer it.

The stack

Three services, one GPU, one lock

A FastAPI service, a browser viewer, and a static front end run as separate deployments on k3s behind Cloudflare, with Keycloak SSO, tenant isolation enforced in the data layer, and per‑plan quotas. Inference runs in a host worker that takes a Postgres advisory lock on the GPU, so a training run and a user upload cannot collide on it.

Exports that load

Into the software that already exists

An RTSTRUCT whose ROI names are capped at 16 characters so that Varian Eclipse accepts them, round‑tripped through an independent reader at median Dice 0.9920. NIfTI on the grid you uploaded, not the canonical frame the network ran in: re‑export the same scan as LPS, RPI, RAS, or PIR, and the labels come back 100.000000% identical. Per‑structure STL in patient coordinates, in millimeters.

The same geometry in two languages, equal to 1e‑9

The implant in the 3D scene on the home page is not an illustration of the product: it is the product’s own implant geometry, running in your browser. The JavaScript that draws an implant in the viewer and the Python that measures one on the server are held to the same map, asserted equal to 1e‑9 on a real GPU, and every drawn vertex is proven to lie inside the measured capsule to within 4×10−6 mm. A number you can see is worth less than a number two independent implementations agree on.

The number

A millimeter, minus the measured model error

Any planner can put a ruler between an implant and a line somebody drew. That line is a prediction, and a prediction has an error: the one measured on the held‑out set above. The error is published, and it is subtracted from the clearance before anything is called safe.

  1. Measured clearance 3.43 mm implant surface to canal surface, in millimeters
  2. The canal’s inward p95 − 0.46 mm how far the drawn canal sits inside the true one, 95th percentile over a 20‑case held‑out set
  3. Graded clearance 2.97 mm what the verdict is computed from
  4. Safety margin to the canal 2.00 mm a graded clearance up to 0.50 mm above this margin is tight, not clear
  5. Verdict CLEAR 0.97 mm of headroom

These are the figures of the 3D scene on the home page, with the implant at the crest. The same arithmetic runs on every structure, each with its own prior: 1.11 mm for the lingual canal, 0.34 mm for a tooth.

Quoted, never subtracted

5.10 mm at the worst point

The p95 is what gets deducted. The single worst inward excursion measured anywhere in that held‑out set was 5.10 mm. It is printed rather than built into the margin, because a number that large is a reason to look at the scan, not a reason to move a threshold.

A refusal is an answer

Not graded outranks tight

When a measurement carries a caveat (the canal drawn in pieces near the site, a saturated distance, no canal at all in the maxilla), no grade is issued. NOT GRADED sorts above TIGHT in the display on purpose: it is the state that most needs somebody to look.

One site, three depths

The same implant, a millimeter apart

A real gap on a held‑out scan: the lower left first molar and second premolar (FDI 36 and 35) are both missing, and the 36 site has 11.9 mm of bone measured above the canal and 6.7 mm of crestal width. An 8 mm implant seated at the crest sits 3.51 mm from the inferior alveolar canal. A millimeter deeper, it is 2.55 mm. Half a millimeter deeper again, it is 2.07 mm, and the plan is refused.

What planning actually does

Seeded from the tooth it replaces

The implant is not dropped on the arch curve at the largest size that fits. It is seeded from the tooth it replaces and seated in the bone that is measured to be there.

Between the cortical plates

Diameter comes from the tooth the site replaces — 3.3 mm for a lateral incisor, 4.8 mm for a first molar — then narrows if the measured ridge cannot take it. The platform is centered between the buccal and lingual plates rather than on the arch spline, because that is where the bone is.

Then an auto‑fit loop measures, steps the length down one catalog size if the canal clearance does not grade clear, and measures again. The implant card says what happened, for example that the implant was shortened two sizes to the longest length that measures clear of the canal, or admits that no length did.

Three axes, and one of them changes nothing

Buccolingual tilt is drawn at its true angle in the cross‑section, and mesiodistal yaw at its true angle on the panoramic view, foreshortened in the section by exactly cos(yaw). That is exact, because the orthogonal projection of a capsule is a capsule.

The third axis is clocking, and the app states plainly that it changes no measurement: the measured solid is a body of revolution. It exists so the connection hex renders where a driver would engage it.

The envelope is the surface the verdict is computed against

It is not a halo or a highlight. The translucent shell around the implant is drawn at 2.46 mm in the mandible (the 2.00 mm margin plus the canal’s own 0.46 mm inward p95), so what you see touching is exactly what the grader compared.

The implant itself is machined titanium in every state. The verdict never lives on the metal, because a reader who learns to read the implant’s color has learned the wrong thing.

Bone density, and what it is not

The site’s density is reported as a ratio: the mean at the osteotomy against this scan’s own cancellous median, both referenced to its air. That makes it affine‑invariant, so it survives a scanner that is not calibrated, and if the scan carries no reference population, no number is produced at all. It is never a Hounsfield number and never a Misch class. CBCT gray values are not calibrated attenuation, so anything that looks like a density measurement is not one.

Before you trust it

Limits of the research

  • Counts are not accuracy. “Twenty‑nine of 32 teeth” is how many labels a model produced on a scan without ground truth, not how many it got right. Accuracy is the held‑out Dice and HD95 above, measured against expert annotation.
  • The checks measure consistency, not correctness. Symmetry, laterality, and component counts catch gross failure and say nothing about whether tooth 26 is really tooth 26. There is no ground truth on a patient scan, so nothing here is an accuracy figure.
  • The same scan twice is not bit-identical. Three runs of one file differ by 108–185 voxels out of 42.8 million — ordinary GPU non-determinism, almost all of it single voxels on a soft-tissue boundary. No numbered tooth differs by more than one voxel, but do not quote these figures to four significant digits.
  • CBCT gray values are not calibrated Hounsfield units. Anything that looks like a density measurement is not one.
  • Nothing is repaired. Quality findings, such as extra components, a tooth sitting in the wrong arch, or asymmetric contralateral volumes, are reported. The exported label map is exactly what the models produced.
  • No surgical guide is produced, and that is a position rather than a gap. A drill guide is a patient‑contacting device that turns a plan into a hole. The canal outline this project measures against is known to sit up to 5.10 mm inside the truth at its worst measured point, and CBCT gray values are not calibrated attenuation. The project publishes the clearance and its error, and stops short of anything that drills.
  • The 3D scene on the home page measures a web mesh, not your scan. Its millimeter is computed in your browser from a decimated, quantized display mesh: an illustration of the method, within about a hundredth of a millimeter of an exact recomputation and checked at both levels of detail. It shows 3D Slicer’s public CBCT sample as segmented by the earlier two‑model pipeline, not by the trained network. The application measures on the voxel grid.
  • Research preview. Not a medical device, not for diagnostic use. Every result requires review by a qualified clinician.
