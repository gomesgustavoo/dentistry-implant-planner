Trained here

The models are ours, and so is the evidence

Seven models sit in the catalogue and five of them are third‑party, labelled as such, with what each is measured to do and whether it is installed at all. Two we trained ourselves — the network that paints all 47 structures, and a second one built for the three canals an anterior implant has to clear.

The base network

nnU‑Net with a U‑Mamba2 bottleneck

Fine‑tuned on ToothFairy3 over 512 cases for 1000 epochs, and scored on a 20‑case holdout it never saw by the same pipeline that serves predictions.

  • Challenge Dice0.8965
  • Strict Dice0.8292
  • HD951.235 mm
  • NSD0.9736
  • Inferior alveolar canal, Dice0.9008 / 0.9009

Above the published SegResNet entry (0.87) and within 0.012 of the ToothFairy3 challenge winners (0.908) — reached on a 12 GB card at 0.36× their patch volume.

The training run

Clean for all 1000 epochs

Validation loss tracked training loss the whole way: no overfitting on 512 cases, and the pseudo‑Dice moving average was still rising at 1000 — not saturated, and the winners trained 1500. checkpoint_final beat checkpoint_best on every metric (0.8887 against 0.8849), which is what a fully annealed poly schedule should do.

A second network, for a reason

The anterior canal specialist

The inferior alveolar canal ends at the mental foramen, so an implant in front of it has three other canals to clear. A network trained on the anterior mandible alone gains +0.0505 / +0.0874 / +0.0172 Dice on the left incisive, right incisive and lingual canals, pulls their predicted volume from 155–205% of ground truth to 99–111%, and beats the base model on 40 external PMCanalSeg cases at p = 7.6 × 10−4.

The finding that was worth the most

A threshold table that was quietly all‑ones

Small‑island removal is per class, against the smallest that structure has ever legitimately been. Rebuilding that table by taking the 2nd percentile over every connected component in the training annotations produced a threshold of one voxel for everything — because ground truth is not clean: the lower jawbone alone has 17,733 components across 512 cases, about 34 per case where the anatomy has one.

Taking the largest component per case instead spans 44 voxels (lingual canal) to 881,756 (lower jawbone). The difference is not cosmetic:

  • Filter inert0.8001 strict · 0.8565 challenge
  • Rebuilt filter0.8292 strict · 0.8965 challenge
Published because it failed

A negative result, with the threshold set first

Three head/neck models were evaluated for an extended taxonomy — 42 more structures beyond the dental set. Across three holdout cases that is 126 structure‑opportunities, and the plausibility gate was committed before anything was measured. One survived it.

The tongue came out at 1.76 cm³ against an anatomical 70–100. One masseter was found and the other was not, inside a field of view containing both. The oropharynx arrived in 155 connected pieces. All three models ship off, and the extended pass is structurally incapable of moving a clearance: it paints only where the dental labels are empty, and a check raises if it ever does otherwise.

What a holdout cannot tell you

Stated before the numbers, because it decides what they mean: this holdout cannot grant ownership of a structure to us or to anyone else — the third‑party models trained on the same public release, so only a loss is evidence. Measured that way over 18 cases, TotalSegmentator took ownership of nothing: zero accessory‑canal voxels in 18 of 18, pulp −0.078 Dice, and the two inferior alveolar canals −0.014 and −0.021.

The stack

Three services, one GPU, one lock

A FastAPI service, a browser viewer and a static front end run as separate deployments on k3s behind Cloudflare, with Keycloak SSO, tenant isolation enforced in the data layer and per‑plan quotas. Inference runs in a host worker that takes a Postgres advisory lock for the card, so a training run and a user upload cannot collide on it.

Exports that load

Into the software that already exists

An RTSTRUCT whose ROI names are capped at 16 characters so Varian Eclipse will take them, round‑tripped through an independent reader at median Dice 0.9920. NIfTI on the grid you uploaded, not the canonical frame the network ran in: re‑export the same scan as LPS, RPI, RAS or PIR and the labels come back 100.000000% identical. Per‑structure STL in patient millimetres.

The same geometry, in two languages, to 1e‑9

The implant you are looking at above is not an illustration of the product — it is the product’s own geometry, running in your browser. The code that draws a fixture in the viewer and the Python that measures one on the server are held to the same map, asserted equal to 1e‑9 on a real GPU, and every drawn vertex is proven to sit inside the measured capsule to 4×10−6 mm. A number a reader can see is worth less than a number two independent implementations agree on.

The number

A millimetre, minus what we know we get wrong

Any planner can put a ruler between an implant and a line somebody drew. The line is a prediction, and a prediction has an error — the one measured on the holdout above. It is published, and it is taken off the clearance before anything is called safe.

  1. Measured clearance 3.43 mm implant surface to canal surface, in patient millimetres
  2. The canal’s inward p95 − 0.46 mm how far the drawn canal sits inside the true one, 95th percentile over a 20‑case held‑out set
  3. Graded clearance 2.97 mm what the verdict is computed from
  4. Safety margin to the nerve 2.00 mm and 0.50 mm of headroom below that is tight, not clear
  5. Verdict CLEAR 0.97 mm of headroom

The figures above are the hero’s, at the crest. The same arithmetic runs on every structure, with that structure’s own prior: 1.11 mm for the lingual canal, 0.34 mm for a tooth.

Quoted, never subtracted

5.10 mm at the worst point

The p95 is what gets deducted. The single worst inward excursion measured anywhere in that holdout was 5.10 mm, and it is printed rather than built into the margin — because a number that large is a reason to look at the scan, not a reason to move a threshold.

A refusal is an answer

Not graded outranks tight

When a measurement carries a caveat — the canal drawn in pieces near the site, a saturated distance, no canal at all in the maxilla — no grade is issued. NOT GRADED sorts above TIGHT in the display on purpose: it is the state that most needs somebody to look.

One site, three depths

The same implant, a millimetre apart

A real gap — a missing lower‑left first molar with 11.9 mm of bone measured above the canal and 6.7 mm of crestal width. An 8 mm fixture seated on the crest sits 3.51 mm off the inferior alveolar canal. A millimetre deeper it is 2.55. Half a millimetre deeper again it is 2.07, and the plan is refused.

What planning actually does

Seeded from the tooth it replaces

The fixture is not dropped on the arch curve at the largest size that fits. It is seeded as a restoration and seated in the bone that is measured to be there.

Between the cortical plates

Diameter comes from the tooth the site replaces — 3.3 mm for a lateral incisor, 4.8 mm for a first molar — then narrows if the measured ridge cannot take it. The platform is centred between the buccal and lingual plates rather than on the arch spline, because that is where the bone is.

Then an auto‑fit loop measures, steps the length down one catalogue size if the canal clearance does not grade clear, and measures again. The card says which happened — “shortened 2 sizes — the longest length that measures clear of the canal here” — or admits that none did.

Three axes, and one of them changes nothing

Buccolingual tilt is drawn at its true angle in the cross‑section; mesiodistal yaw at its true angle on the panoramic, and foreshortened in the section by exactly cos(yaw) — exact, because the orthogonal projection of a capsule is a capsule.

The third is clocking, and it is stated plainly to change no measurement: the measured solid is a body of revolution. It exists so the connection hex renders where a driver would engage it.

The envelope is the surface the verdict is computed against

Not a halo and not a highlight. The translucent shell around the fixture is drawn at 2.46 mm in the mandible — the 2.00 mm margin plus the canal’s own 0.46 mm inward p95 — so what you see touching is what the grader compared.

The fixture itself is machined titanium in every state. The verdict never lives on the metal, because a reader who learns to read the implant’s colour has learned the wrong thing.

Bone density, and what it is not

The site’s density is reported as a ratio: the mean at the osteotomy against this scan’s own cancellous median, both referenced to its air. That makes it affine‑invariant, so it survives a scanner that is not calibrated — and if the scan carries no reference population, no number is produced at all. It is never a Hounsfield number and never a Misch class. CBCT grey values are not calibrated attenuation, so anything that looks like a density measurement is not one.

Before you trust it

What we do not claim

  • None of these numbers are accuracy. The example cases are public sample data with no ground truth. "29 of 32 teeth" is how many labels the model produced, not how many it got right.
  • The checks are consistency, not correctness. Symmetry, laterality and component counts catch gross failure and say nothing about whether tooth 26 is really tooth 26. There is no ground truth on a patient scan, so nothing here is an accuracy figure.
  • The same scan twice is not bit-identical. Three runs of one file differ by 108–185 voxels out of 42.8 million — ordinary GPU non-determinism, almost all of it single voxels on a soft-tissue boundary. No numbered tooth differs by more than one voxel, but do not quote these figures to four significant digits.
  • CBCT grey values are not calibrated Hounsfield units. Anything that looks like a density measurement is not one.
  • Nothing is repaired. Quality findings — extra components, a tooth sitting in the wrong arch, asymmetric contralateral volumes — are reported. The exported label map is exactly what the models produced.
  • No surgical guide is produced, and that is a position rather than a gap. A drill guide is a patient-contacting device that converts a plan into a hole. The canal outline this product measures against is known to sit up to 5.10 mm inside the truth at its worst measured point, and CBCT grey values are not calibrated attenuation. We publish the clearance and its error; we do not hand you something that drills.
  • The hero on this page measures a web mesh, not your scan. Its millimetre is computed in your browser from a decimated, quantised display mesh — an illustration of the method, honest to about a hundredth of a millimetre against an exact recomputation, and checked at both levels of detail. The product measures on the voxel grid.
  • Research preview. Not a medical device, not for diagnostic use. A clinician reads every result.

Reference

Questions worth asking

What can I upload?

A DICOM series as a .zip, or a single-file volume: .nii / .nii.gz. Upload DICOM if you can — the RTSTRUCT then references your own series, which is what makes it drop straight into a planning system.

There is a hard 100 MB ceiling per upload at our edge, which a 0.25 mm full-head CBCT can exceed. Chunked upload for larger scans is not built yet, and the app tells you before it wastes your time rather than after.

Whose models are these?

Ours, finetuned from someone else’s. The architecture is U-Mamba2 (Wu et al., MICCAI 2025), which won both ToothFairy3 tasks; we finetuned it on the ToothFairy3 challenge dataset — 512 annotated CBCT volumes from the University of Modena and Reggio Emilia — and that checkpoint is what runs here. The service used to run two published models instead; it does not any more.

Because the training data is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, so is the model: attribution, share-alike, and non-commercial use only. This is a research preview and is licensed as one.

Does the orientation of my scan matter?

No, and this is worth being specific about because it is the failure everyone hits. The model numbers teeth by position, so a scan in the wrong frame gets every tooth mirrored — 11 becomes 21, left becomes right. Every upload is canonicalised first, including scans with a real patient tilt. Re-export the same scan as LPS, RPI, RAS or PIR and the labels come back 100.000000% identical over 4.8 million voxels.

How long does a scan take?

About 98 seconds of GPU for the two models that run — 95.0 s for the base network and 2.5 s for the anterior canal specialist, both figures published in the model catalogue before you upload anything — plus roughly a minute of CPU for the merge, the quality checks, the meshes and the viewer tiles. There is one GPU, so a job may queue behind another; the app shows you where you are.

What happens to my data?

The upload is deleted the moment a job reaches done — it is never read again. Results expire 72 hours after completion and the endpoint then answers 410 Gone rather than a 404 that would read like a bug. Each account's cases are stored under their own prefix and no account can see another's.

Can I trust the clearance it reports?

Treat it as a first pass that a clinician checks, which is what the limits section is for. It is a research preview, not a medical device, and it is not for diagnostic use. The useful question is not whether it is perfect but whether checking its canal is faster than tracing one — and the report tells you where it is unsure.

How is the clearance actually computed?

The measured solid is a capsule — the fixture’s axis segment, swept at its radius, with a hemispherical apex. The clearance is the nearest distance from that segment to the canal surface, less the radius, on the full-resolution grid you uploaded. The axis span is the length minus the radius, not the length: walking a full-length cylinder costs up to one radius of false conservatism and used to suppress the verdict on every vertical implant over a canal.

Then the structure’s own inward p95 is subtracted, and the result is compared to the margin. Both numbers, and the arithmetic, are on the printed plan sheet under “how every figure above was obtained”.

Does it produce a surgical guide?

No, and it will not. A guide is a patient-contacting device; this is a research preview that publishes a measurement and its error. The canal outline it measures against sat up to 5.10 mm inside the truth at the worst single point of a 20-case held-out set, and CBCT grey values are not calibrated attenuation. You get the clearance, the error budget behind it, and an STL of the planned fixture. What you do with them is a clinical decision.

What counts as one segmentation?

One scan through the full pipeline, with every export produced. Re-running the same scan counts again. A job that fails does not count, and a job you cancel before it reaches the GPU does not count either.
